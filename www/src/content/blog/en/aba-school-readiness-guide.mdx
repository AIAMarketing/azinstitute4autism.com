Antecedent interventions in ABA change what happens before a behavior to make useful behaviors more likely or reduce conditions associated with interfering behavior. Consequence strategies focus on what happens after a behavior and how that outcome affects future behavior. Effective strategies are individualized and should account for the behavior's function, context, and the learner's needs.

If you are a parent, the terminology can sound more complicated than the underlying idea. ABA often looks at what happened **before** a behavior, what the person **did**, and what happened **afterward**. Understanding that sequence can help adults identify barriers, teach more effective ways to communicate or participate, and create environments that better support success.

The goal is not simply to stop behavior that adults find difficult. A more useful question is what the behavior may be accomplishing, what the learner needs in that situation, and whether changing the environment, teaching a new skill, or changing what follows the behavior could help.

<h2 id="what-are-antecedents-and-consequences-in-aba-">What are antecedents and consequences in ABA?</h2>

![ABC model showing a parent giving a transition warning, a child requesting more time, and the parent granting two additional minutes as the consequence.](/assets/images/antecedent-behavior-consequence.webp)

Antecedents and consequences are two parts of the ABC model used in Applied Behavior Analysis.

The [ABC model](https://iris.peabody.vanderbilt.edu/module/bp/cresource/q1/p02/) looks at three parts of a behavioral event:

<table><thead><tr><th>Part</th><th>What it means</th><th>Simple example</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td><strong>Antecedent</strong></td><td>What happens before the behavior</td><td>A parent says tablet time is ending in two minutes</td></tr><tr><td><strong>Behavior</strong></td><td>What the person does in observable terms</td><td>The child hands over the tablet and asks for more time</td></tr><tr><td><strong>Consequence</strong></td><td>What happens immediately afterward</td><td>The parent acknowledges the request and offers another brief turn later</td></tr></tbody></table>

The ABC sequence does not mean that every antecedent directly "causes" a behavior or that one observation reveals why the behavior occurred. Sleep, illness, sensory discomfort, previous events, task difficulty, communication needs, social interactions, and many other factors can affect what happens.

Instead, ABC information helps clinicians and caregivers look for patterns.

An **antecedent intervention** changes something before the behavior. A **consequence strategy** changes or plans what happens afterward.

Both can be useful, but neither should be chosen simply because a strategy appears on a list.

<h2 id="what-are-antecedent-interventions-in-aba-">What are antecedent interventions in ABA?</h2>

Antecedent interventions are proactive strategies that change an event, expectation, environment, cue, or condition that occurs before a behavior.

For example, if a child regularly becomes distressed when an activity ends without warning, an antecedent strategy might provide a visual timer or advance notice before the transition. If a long worksheet consistently leads to frustration, the task might be broken into shorter sections or presented differently.

The IRIS Center describes [antecedent-based interventions](https://iris.peabody.vanderbilt.edu/module/bip-elem/cresource/q1/p06/) as changes to conditions that commonly precede interfering behavior. Examples include changes to the physical environment, visual supports, prompts, task adaptations, choice, regulation supports, and scheduled access to reinforcement.

The important word is **individualized**. A visual schedule may be extremely helpful for one learner and irrelevant to the concern another learner is experiencing.

<h3 id="common-examples-of-antecedent-strategies-in-aba">Common examples of antecedent strategies in ABA</h3>

Antecedent interventions can range from very simple environmental changes to more formal procedures developed as part of a behavior plan.

<table><thead><tr><th>Antecedent strategy</th><th>What changes before behavior</th><th>Example</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td><strong>Visual supports</strong></td><td>Information becomes more concrete and predictable</td><td>A visual schedule shows the sequence of morning activities</td></tr><tr><td><strong>Advance notice or priming</strong></td><td>The learner receives information before a change or demand</td><td>Reviewing what will happen before a new appointment</td></tr><tr><td><strong>Choice-making</strong></td><td>The learner has meaningful control within an activity</td><td>Choosing whether to use a pencil or marker</td></tr><tr><td><strong>Task modification</strong></td><td>The size, difficulty, format, or presentation of a task changes</td><td>Breaking a long assignment into shorter sections</td></tr><tr><td><strong>Environmental modification</strong></td><td>Sensory or physical conditions change</td><td>Moving to a quieter workspace when noise interferes with participation</td></tr><tr><td><strong>Prompting or pre-correction</strong></td><td>A useful response is cued before it is needed</td><td>Showing a break card before beginning a difficult activity</td></tr><tr><td><strong>Scheduled access or breaks</strong></td><td>A need is met predictably rather than only after difficulty occurs</td><td>Building regular movement breaks into a long routine</td></tr></tbody></table>

The same strategy should not automatically be used for everyone who demonstrates the same behavior. Two children may both leave a table, for example, while one is escaping a task that is too difficult and another is moving away from overwhelming noise.

Understanding that difference matters.

<h3 id="visual-schedules-and-other-visual-supports">Visual schedules and other visual supports</h3>

Visual schedules are one of the most recognizable antecedent interventions. They make information available in a form the learner can continue to reference rather than relying only on spoken instructions.

A schedule might show:

**Breakfast → Brush teeth → Shoes → Backpack → Car**

For another learner, the appropriate support may be a written checklist, calendar, phone reminder, color-coded planner, or simple First/Then card.

AIA's guide to [AAC and visual supports](/library/aac-visual-supports-autism) explores how different visual tools can support communication, predictability, and independence.

Visual supports should make information more accessible. They should not become a tool for demanding rigid adherence when flexibility or accommodation would better meet the learner's needs.

<h3 id="first-then-supports">First/Then supports</h3>

A First/Then support shows a short sequence:

**First:** Put shoes on.  
**Then:** Choose music for the car.

This can clarify what is happening now and what will happen next. For some learners, the "then" activity may also function as reinforcement.

AIA's [First/Then Cards guide](/library/first-then-cards-autism-transitions) includes additional examples for home, school, and community routines.

First/Then should not be interpreted as "do whatever the adult demands to earn something." The task should be appropriate, supports should be available, and the learner should have meaningful ways to communicate help, discomfort, or the need for a break.

<h3 id="choice-as-an-antecedent-strategy">Choice as an antecedent strategy</h3>

Choice can change how a situation is experienced before behavior occurs.

Instead of:

"Sit here and do this worksheet."

an adult might say:

"Would you like to work at the table or the desk?"

or:

"Do you want to start with reading or math?"

The requirement itself may not always be optional, but aspects of how it is completed often can be.

Meaningful choices can support autonomy and give adults valuable information about preferences. A child repeatedly choosing the quieter workspace, for example, may be telling us something important about the environment.

<h3 id="task-and-environmental-modification">Task and environmental modification</h3>

Sometimes the most effective intervention is changing what adults are asking or where they are asking it.

A task may be too difficult, too long, visually overwhelming, poorly explained, or mismatched to the skill being assessed. An environment may be too loud, crowded, bright, distracting, or unpredictable.

Possible antecedent modifications might include breaking a task into smaller steps, presenting fewer items at once, allowing an appropriate alternate response format, reducing competing distractions, changing seating, or adding clearer visual information.

This does not mean eliminating every difficult task. It means asking whether the current setup is creating an unnecessary barrier to learning.

<h2 id="what-is-antecedent-manipulation-in-aba-">What is antecedent manipulation in ABA?</h2>

**Antecedent manipulation** is another term used for intentionally changing something that occurs before behavior in order to influence what happens next.

The word "manipulation" here refers to modifying an environmental variable, not manipulating a person.

For example, a behavior analyst might change the timing of a demand, provide a visual cue, alter task difficulty, schedule access to a break, or change the physical environment after assessment suggests that doing so addresses a relevant antecedent.

In everyday parent conversations, **antecedent intervention**, **antecedent strategy**, and **antecedent modification** are often easier phrases to understand.

<h2 id="what-are-consequence-strategies-in-aba-">What are consequence strategies in ABA?</h2>

A consequence in ABA is simply an event or change that follows a behavior. It does **not** automatically mean a punishment or something unpleasant.

Consequences matter because what happens after a behavior can affect how likely that behavior is to occur again under similar circumstances.

For example, imagine that a child appropriately asks, "Can I have help?" during a difficult task. The adult responds promptly and helps them. If receiving useful assistance makes the child more likely to ask for help again in the future, that consequence has reinforced the help request.

This is why behavior analysts pay close attention not only to challenging behavior, but also to what happens after communication, coping, independence, self-advocacy, and other useful skills.

<h3 id="reinforcement-and-replacement-behavior">Reinforcement and replacement behavior</h3>

**Reinforcement** means that something happening after a behavior increases the future likelihood of that behavior.

That definition depends on what actually happens over time, not simply what the adult intended.

![Infographic comparing two responses to a difficult task: one path shows a child pushing work away and escaping the task, while the other shows the child requesting help and receiving support to continue learning.](/assets/images/reinforcement-replacement-behavior.webp)

Praise may reinforce one child's behavior but make little difference to another. A short movement break, conversation, music, access to an activity, assistance, extra time, or another experience may be more meaningful depending on the person and situation.

A useful behavior plan often focuses on a **replacement behavior**, meaning a safer or more effective way for the learner to meet the same need.

For example:

A child finds a task too difficult and has historically pushed materials away.

A replacement skill might be handing over a "help" card, using AAC to request assistance, saying "break," or asking for the task to be explained differently.

If adults reliably respond to that useful communication, the learner discovers that the replacement skill works.

This is one reason functional communication is so important. Communication gives learners more effective ways to influence their environment.

<h3 id="differential-reinforcement">Differential reinforcement</h3>

Differential reinforcement is a broader family of ABA procedures in which reinforcement is provided for a selected behavior while the treatment plan changes reinforcement for another behavior.

One common example is reinforcing an appropriate alternative behavior.

Suppose a child has learned to tap an adult repeatedly to gain attention. The team may teach a more effective attention request and make sure that appropriate request reliably produces attention.

The important part is not simply "reward the good behavior." The replacement behavior should actually work for the learner and address the relevant need.

<h3 id="what-about-ignoring-behavior-">What about ignoring behavior?</h3>

"Ignore it" is not a safe universal consequence strategy.

Withholding attention would only make behavioral sense when attention is actually maintaining the behavior, and even then the full plan must consider safety, communication, replacement skills, feasibility, and possible side effects.

A crying child may be in pain. A learner covering their ears may be overwhelmed by noise. A child leaving a task may not understand it. Aggression or self-injury may require immediate safety responses.

Behavior should be understood in context before adults decide what consequence procedure is appropriate.

For significant, persistent, or unsafe behavior, an individualized assessment and plan from qualified professionals is more appropriate than experimenting with extinction or planned ignoring at home.

<h2 id="antecedent-vs-consequence-strategies-what-is-the-difference-">Antecedent vs. consequence strategies: What is the difference?</h2>

The simplest difference is timing.

**Antecedent strategies change what happens before behavior. Consequence strategies address what happens after behavior.**

<table><thead><tr><th>&nbsp;</th><th>Antecedent strategies</th><th>Consequence strategies</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td><strong>When they occur</strong></td><td>Before behavior</td><td>After behavior</td></tr><tr><td><strong>Primary question</strong></td><td>What could we change beforehand?</td><td>What happens after this behavior?</td></tr><tr><td><strong>Examples</strong></td><td>Visual schedules, choice, priming, task modification, environmental changes</td><td>Reinforcement of communication, independence, coping, or other replacement skills</td></tr><tr><td><strong>Purpose</strong></td><td>Change the conditions under which behavior occurs</td><td>Affect the future likelihood of behavior</td></tr><tr><td><strong>Should selection consider function?</strong></td><td>Yes</td><td>Yes</td></tr></tbody></table>

In practice, effective support often uses both.

A child who becomes distressed when leaving the playground might receive a five-minute warning and visual timer before the transition. Those are antecedent supports.

If the child then appropriately asks for one final turn and the adult responds according to the agreed plan, what happens after that communication is part of the consequence.

The two sides of the ABC sequence work together.

> ABC observations identify patterns, not certainty. What happens before and after behavior can provide useful clues, but behavioral function should not be declared from a single observation.

<h2 id="why-the-function-of-behavior-matters">Why the function of behavior matters</h2>

The same behavior can occur for very different reasons.

A child may leave a group because the room is painfully loud. Another may leave because the activity is too difficult. Another may be trying to reach a preferred toy. Another may have learned that leaving reliably produces one-on-one adult interaction.

That is why behavior analysts look beyond what the behavior looks like.

AIA's guide to the [four functions of behavior](/library/behavior-management-functions-guide) provides a parent-friendly overview of common functional categories.

Function should not be guessed from one incident.

For more significant behavioral concerns, a [Functional Behavioral Assessment](https://iris.peabody.vanderbilt.edu/module/fba-elem/cwrap/) uses multiple information sources and systematic observation to identify patterns in antecedents, consequences, setting events, and behavior. The resulting information helps professionals develop a hypothesis about why the behavior is occurring and select function-based support.

<h3 id="what-are-setting-events-">What are setting events?</h3>

Not everything affecting behavior happens immediately beforehand.

A **setting event** is a condition or earlier experience that changes how likely a behavior may be in a later situation.

Poor sleep, illness, a stressful interaction earlier in the day, hunger, changes in routine, or other circumstances may alter how a learner responds to something that is usually manageable.

For example, a difficult worksheet might regularly be completed with support. After a night of very little sleep, the same worksheet may be much harder to tolerate.

Noticing setting events prevents adults from reducing every behavior to a single immediate "trigger."

<h2 id="abc-data-can-identify-patterns-but-it-is-not-a-diagnosis">ABC data can identify patterns, but it is not a diagnosis</h2>

Parents and educators can sometimes learn useful information by recording:

**A:** What happened before?

**B:** What did the person do?

**C:** What happened afterward?

Over multiple observations, patterns may emerge.

However, one ABC note cannot establish a behavioral function, and even repeated ABC observations are only one source of information that may be used in a more complete assessment.

This distinction matters. A behavior that appears to be "attention seeking" could involve communication difficulty, uncertainty, anxiety, sensory discomfort, pain, task difficulty, or several interacting variables.

The goal of observation is to become more curious and precise, not more certain than the data justify.

<h2 id="how-antecedent-and-consequence-strategies-fit-with-proactive-and-reactive-aba">How antecedent and consequence strategies fit with proactive and reactive ABA</h2>

Antecedent strategies are usually proactive because they change conditions before a target behavior occurs.

Consequence strategies occur afterward, but that does not mean every consequence strategy is a crisis response. Reinforcing an appropriate request for help is a consequence strategy, for example, even though nothing unsafe or disruptive occurred.

Likewise, proactive ABA involves much more than antecedent manipulation. It can include teaching communication, coping, self-advocacy, daily living, and other skills before difficult moments occur.

For a deeper comparison, see AIA's guide to [proactive and reactive ABA strategies](/library/proactive-reactive-aba-strategies-guide).

<h2 id="can-parents-use-antecedent-strategies-at-home-">Can parents use antecedent strategies at home?</h2>

Parents already change antecedents and consequences every day, often without using ABA terminology.

Giving a warning before leaving the park, putting tomorrow's clothes out the night before, writing a grocery list, choosing a quieter restaurant table, helping when a child asks appropriately, or breaking a difficult chore into smaller steps all change environmental conditions around behavior.

The difference between an everyday support and an individualized behavioral intervention becomes more important when behavior is severe, persistent, unsafe, or difficult to understand.

For everyday routines, ask practical questions:

**What seems to make this situation harder?**

**Can the expectation or environment be made clearer?**

**Does my child have an effective way to ask for help, a break, more information, or a change?**

**What happens when they use that communication successfully?**

Those questions often lead to more useful support than simply asking, "How do I stop this behavior?"

<h2 id="when-should-a-bcba-be-involved-">When should a BCBA be involved?</h2>

Parents do not need a formal behavior plan for every difficult morning or transition.

Professional support becomes more important when behavior is unsafe, significantly interferes with daily life or learning, persists despite reasonable adjustments, changes suddenly without a clear explanation, or requires procedures that depend on identifying behavioral function.

The Behavior Analyst Certification Board describes [Board Certified Behavior Analysts](https://www.bacb.com/bcba/) as independent practitioners who can provide behavior-analytic services and supervise professionals implementing behavior-analytic interventions.

A qualified behavior analyst can gather data, collaborate with the learner and family, consider environmental and skill-related barriers, identify meaningful goals, and determine whether antecedent changes, skill teaching, consequence strategies, or a combination are appropriate.

Behavior that may reflect pain, illness, mental health concerns, sensory difficulties, communication needs, or another medical or developmental issue may also require collaboration with other appropriate professionals.

<h2 id="individualized-aba-support-at-aia">Individualized ABA support at AIA</h2>

At Arizona Institute for Autism, understanding behavior begins with the individual.

Our goal is not simply to make a behavior disappear. We consider what the learner may be communicating, what environmental conditions are affecting participation, what skills could increase independence, and how adults can respond in ways that support meaningful progress.

Antecedent interventions and consequence strategies are most useful when they are part of an individualized, data-informed approach that respects communication, autonomy, safety, and each learner's needs.
